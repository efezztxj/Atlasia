=== prytaneion ===
North of the State Agora stands the Prytaneion, an important place in the civic and religious life of Ephesus. It contained the city's sacred hearth. Priests known as the Curetes tended its fire, and their names were inscribed on the building's columns. Statues of Artemis of Ephesus found here during excavations are now displayed in the Ephesus Museum in Selçuk. The re-erected columns help us imagine the building's former appearance.

=== devlet-agorasi ===
Also known as the Upper Agora, this area was one of the centres of public life in Ephesus. Unlike the Commercial Agora, it was associated with official and civic gatherings. The square was laid out over an older burial ground. The covered, colonnaded galleries around it were called stoas. The Basilica Stoa to the north and the nearby public buildings reveal its importance in the city's civic life.

=== basilica-stoa ===
This great colonnaded building ran along the northern side of the State Agora, framing the city's public space. In the year eleven CE, Gaius Sextilius Pollio and his family dedicated it to Artemis, the emperors and the city. Bull heads carved into the Ionic column capitals were among its striking details. The long hall offered shelter from rain and sun and was also associated with public affairs and meetings.

=== celsus-kutuphanesi ===
The Library of Celsus is one of the best-known monuments of Ephesus. In the second century CE, Aquila had it built in memory of his father, the senator Celsus. It was more than a library: the burial chamber of Celsus lay beneath it. Inside were niches and shelves for manuscript scrolls. The two-storey façade visible today was re-erected in the twentieth century using excavated fragments and additional materials. Four statues on the façade represent wisdom, virtue, knowledge and intelligence; the original statues are now in Vienna.

=== buyuk-tiyatro ===
The Great Theatre of Ephesus is a monumental open-air structure set into the slope of Panayır Mountain. Its origins reach back to the Hellenistic period, and it was enlarged to its great size under Roman rule. The seating tiers and remains of the stage building convey the scale of this place where communities gathered. Sources give different estimates for its capacity, so an exact figure is difficult to establish. Across from the theatre, Harbour Street once led towards the ancient harbour. Today the remains help us understand the many layers of Ephesian history.

=== hadrian-tapinagi ===
This elegant building on Curetes Street is commonly known as the Temple of Hadrian. The name does not mean its precise function is beyond debate: researchers have studied its role in the imperial cult and its relationship to the baths behind it. Its arch, columns and plant motifs draw attention. Dating to the second century, the façade was re-erected in the twentieth century using excavated fragments. The originals of the reliefs displayed at the front are preserved in the Ephesus Museum; copies are shown here. This small monument offers rich details for understanding the architecture of Ephesus.

=== trajan-cesmesi ===
The Fountain of Trajan stood on Curetes Street as a monumental display of the city's water supply. An inscription records that the fountain was dedicated to Artemis and Emperor Trajan by the benefactor Tiberius Claudius Aristion and his wife. The basin was framed by an ornate, two-storey façade. A colossal statue of the emperor once stood above the water. Today surviving architectural fragments help us imagine how water, sculpture and civic generosity came together in the public life of Ephesus.

=== yamac-evler ===
The Terrace Houses are an important archaeological site for understanding domestic life in Roman Ephesus. The houses were built on terraces along the slope of Bülbül Mountain. In the area known as Terrace House Two, wall paintings, mosaics and marble revetments have survived in homes arranged around courtyards. Water installations and traces of underfloor heating reveal the comforts available in some homes. Excavation and conservation have included reassembling fragmented decoration. A protective roof now helps shelter these fragile remains. Check official information before visiting, as access conditions can change.

=== odeon ===
North of the State Agora, this small theatre-shaped building is known as the Odeon or Bouleuterion. Bouleuterion means a meeting place for the city council. The building may also have hosted performances. Stone seating, an orchestra area and a stage building give it the appearance of a small theatre. Inscriptions connect Vedius Antoninus and his family with the building in the second century, but evidence also points to an earlier council house. It is therefore better to think of these remains as a civic space renewed over time, rather than a building created once and never changed.

=== ticaret-agorasi ===
The Commercial Agora was a marketplace near the ancient harbour of Ephesus. Its almost square plan also gave it the name Tetragonos Agora. The area goes back to the Hellenistic period; expansion under Augustus and later alterations changed its appearance. Behind the colonnaded galleries surrounding the open courtyard were shops and workspaces. Gates connected the market to the city's streets. The nearby Library of Celsus and the Gate of Mazaeus and Mithridates help us understand this district within the ancient city. The map marker represents an approximate point, not the boundaries of the entire agora.

=== mazaeus-mithridates ===
The Gate of Mazaeus and Mithridates stands beside the Library of Celsus, at the southern entrance to the Commercial Agora. Inscriptions on the three-arched monument record that it was commissioned by the freedmen Mazaeus and Mithridates in honour of Augustus and his family. The titles in the inscription help date it to around 4–3 BCE. The gate was associated with the arrangement of the agora in antiquity and was re-erected in the twentieth century. Its inscriptions illuminate the city's social relationships as well as its architecture.

=== memmius ===
The Memmius Monument near Curetes Street is an honorary monument from the Roman period. Sources relate that Gaius Memmius commissioned it in connection with his family, and that the Roman dictator Sulla was his grandfather. Architectural fragments and figurative decoration can be seen among the remains. Reconstructions of its original appearance involve interpretation, so the present arrangement should not be treated as a certain image of the ancient building. The nearby Hydreion is a separate water-related structure. The marker approximately represents the area where the two remains stand close together.

=== skolastika ===
The Baths of Scholastica near Curetes Street were a Roman complex used for bathing, relaxation and social gatherings. Sources describe an early Roman foundation and a late antique renovation by a woman named Scholastica. The plan included changing, cold, warm and hot rooms. An underfloor heating system helps us understand how the heated spaces worked. The remains reflect interventions from different periods; the walls visible today were not all built at the same time. The nearby latrine, or communal toilet, was a separate public facility.

=== meryem-kilisesi ===
The Church of Mary is a large church complex in the northwestern part of ancient Ephesus. It developed within the remains of an earlier Roman building and offers a glimpse into the city's late antique history. A council met at Ephesus in 431, but scholars debate its exact venue and the different phases of the building. This is not the same place as the House of the Virgin Mary outside the ancient city.

=== efes-stadyumu ===
The Stadium of Ephesus stretches across the northern part of the ancient city, near the slopes of Mount Panayır. This elongated space was used for various spectacles and competitions in the Roman period. Remains from different phases of its history can still be seen. The map marker represents the wider stadium area; it does not indicate a visitor entrance or walking route.

=== artemision ===
The Temple of Artemis was part of a great sanctuary dedicated to Artemis of Ephesus. It was celebrated as one of the seven wonders of the ancient world. The remains visible today lie near Selçuk, separate from the main ancient city. The temple was rebuilt in different periods, so its surviving remains should not all be assigned to a single phase.

=== aziz-yuhanna ===
The Basilica of Saint John stands on Ayasuluk Hill in Selçuk. The remains visible today belong to a large Byzantine church built in the sixth century during the reign of Emperor Justinian. Christian tradition associates the site with the tomb of John the Apostle; this association should be understood as a tradition. The basilica lies in the Ayasuluk component of the Ephesus World Heritage site, separate from the ancient city core.

=== isa-bey ===
The İsa Bey Mosque stands on the western slope of Ayasuluk Hill in Selçuk, between the Temple of Artemis and the Basilica of Saint John. It was commissioned for İsa Bey in the fourteenth century, during the Aydınid period. Its spacious courtyard and stonework are among its notable features. It is separate from the ancient city core, and the map marker does not indicate the visitor entrance.

=== oktagon ===
The Octagon is an eight-sided funerary monument on Curetes Street, near the Hellenistic Heroon. For many years it was associated with Arsinoe, the sister of Cleopatra. However, a scientific study published in 2025 found that the examined remains belonged to a young male and could not be Arsinoe's. This result does not, on its own, explain every phase of the monument's use and burials.

=== heroon ===
The Hellenistic Heroon is a monument near the Octagon, at the lower end of Curetes Street. The word heroon describes a monument associated with a hero cult. The structure is sometimes linked to Androclus, the legendary founder of Ephesus, but this identification is uncertain. The remains and proposed reconstructions help researchers explore the Hellenistic history of the city.

=== latrina ===
The latrine at Ephesus was a shared toilet facility in the ancient city. It stands near the bath buildings at the lower end of Curetes Street. Its seating and the channel beneath provide a glimpse of water and waste management in Roman urban life. The remains seen today should be considered alongside changes made to the building over time.

=== liman-caddesi ===
Harbour Street is a broad avenue running from the Great Theatre towards the ancient harbour. It is also known as the Arcadiane and is associated with renovations in the reign of Emperor Arcadius in late antiquity. Colonnaded walkways and surrounding buildings evoke the impressive urban space encountered by people arriving from the harbour. The map marker represents only one point along this long street.

=== domitian-meydani ===
Domitian Square was a public space in the upper city of Ephesus, near the terrace of an imperial cult temple. The monuments and water structures around it help us understand civic life in different periods of the city's history. The square's modern name is associated with the temple. The map marker represents the wider area, not its boundaries or an entrance.

=== yedi-uyuyanlar ===
The Cave of the Seven Sleepers and its surrounding burial ground lie on the slopes of Mount Panayır, outside the ancient city core of Ephesus. The site contains rock-cut tombs and the remains of a church. It is one of several places associated with the story of young people who awoke after a long sleep. Christian and Islamic traditions tell the story in different ways; the identification of this cave with the legend is not an established historical fact. Check current access separately.

=== ayasuluk ===
Ayasuluk Hill in Selçuk brings together traces from different periods of the long history of Ephesus. Evidence of early settlement has been found on the hill; fortifications and the Basilica of Saint John later developed here. The walls of the hilltop fortress were built and repaired in different periods. Ayasuluk is part of the Ephesus World Heritage site, in a location separate from the ancient city core.

=== lukas ===
The monument known as the Tomb of Saint Luke is a circular structure near the southern entrance area of Ephesus. Despite its name, its identification as Saint Luke's tomb is not established. According to research by Austrian archaeologist Andreas Pülz, it was originally built as a Roman fountain and adapted for use as a church in the fifth century. Its transformation illustrates how ancient buildings were reused in later periods. The map marker represents the remains, not a visitor entrance.

=== magnesia-kapisi ===
The Magnesian Gate is one of the ancient city gates southeast of Ephesus. It takes its name from the road leading towards Magnesia. The gate and fortifications changed over different periods, and sources disagree on the date of its first construction. This ancient gate is not the same as the modern visitor entrance. The map marker shows an approximate location of the remains; visitor access should be checked separately.

=== herakles-kapisi ===
The Hercules Gate is a monumental passage at the upper end of Curetes Street. It takes its name from the reliefs of Hercules on the two surviving pillars. Sources indicate that the reliefs and the gate's present arrangement may belong to different periods. The narrow passage marked the transition from the upper city onto the street. It should not be confused with the ancient Magnesian Gate or Hadrian's Gate.

=== dogu-gymnasionu ===
The East Gymnasium is a large complex in eastern Ephesus, near the ancient Magnesian Gate. Gymnasiums were associated with physical training and social life, and this complex also included baths. Its surviving remains and excavations help us understand its different sections. The marker represents the wider area; its entrance and current visiting conditions should be checked separately.

=== domitian-tapinagi ===
The building known as the Temple of Domitian was a temple associated with the imperial cult at Ephesus. It stood on a raised terrace beside Domitian Square and is also called the Temple of the Sebastoi, or the emperors. Much of its upper structure has disappeared, but remains of the terrace and substructure can still be seen. Interpretations of individual statues and building phases require care.

=== kuretler-caddesi ===
Curetes Street was one of the main streets linking the upper city of Ephesus with the area around the library. Colonnaded walkways, fountains and monuments lined its sides. Its modern name comes from the Curetes, a group associated with the city's sacred hearth. The street was part of ceremonial routes as well as daily urban life. The marker shows only one point along the long street.

=== mermer-cadde ===
Marble Street is an ancient road running from the area of the Library of Celsus towards the Great Theatre. It follows the eastern side of the Commercial Agora and connects important public spaces of the city. Its name comes from the marble paving. The traces visible today reflect a long period of use and changes over time. The marker represents one point, not the whole street or a walking route.

=== tiyatro-gymnasionu ===
Across from the Great Theatre and north of Harbour Street stood the Theatre Gymnasium, a Roman complex combining exercise spaces and baths. Built in the second century, it was distinct from the Vedius Gymnasium north of the stadium. A colonnaded exercise court, known as a palaestra, formed part of the complex. The remains and scattered architectural fragments help us imagine how bathing and physical training shaped public life. Later changes and the unfinished state of excavation make some details uncertain. The map pin represents the broader complex, not an entrance. Check current access before visiting.

=== yukari-hamam ===
At the eastern end of the State Agora, near the Odeon, are the remains of the Upper Gymnasium and baths. Parts of this large Roman complex stood against the slope of Mount Pion. Baths were places not only for washing but also for meeting and social life. The complex has not been fully investigated, so questions about its plan and history remain. Some sources call it the Baths of Varius, a name also associated with the Baths of Scholastica on Curetes Street. These are distinct sites. The map pin represents the complex approximately; it is not an entrance.

=== serapeion ===
West of the Commercial Agora stands a large sanctuary commonly known as the Temple of Serapis, or the Serapeion. Built during the Roman period, its temple and surrounding architectural remains reflect the monumental character of ancient Ephesus. Despite the familiar name, it is debated whether the sanctuary was actually dedicated to Serapis. Scholars have interpreted inscriptions and finds in different ways and proposed other functions. That uncertainty matters when we look at the surviving remains. The map pin is an approximate centre of the area, not an entrance or an indication of access.

=== yamac-ev-2 ===
Terrace House Two is a residential block built on the slope south of Curetes Street. Separate homes were arranged in terraces to follow the steep ground. Rooms around courtyards preserve wall paintings, mosaics and marble revetments. Water installations and traces of underfloor heating in some rooms offer a glimpse of domestic life. As the Austrian Archaeological Institute explains, the paintings and mosaics were left in place and a protective roof was erected over the site. This stop focuses on one part of the broader Terrace Houses already in the tour. The pin marks an approximate centre, not an entrance. Check current visiting conditions before you go.

=== liman-hamamlari ===
The Harbour Baths and Gymnasium formed a large Roman complex in the old harbour district of Ephesus, north of Harbour Street. Baths were combined with spaces for exercise and education. Its earliest phases belong to the Roman imperial period; the complex was later damaged and repaired at different times. The nearby Halls of Verulanus and their broad court were associated with athletic and public life in this area. Only part of the remains has been excavated. The map pin marks the approximate centre of the wider complex, not a visitor entrance or a safe walking route. Check current access before visiting.

=== meryem-ana-evi ===
The House of the Virgin Mary is a shrine on Bülbül Mountain, outside the core of ancient Ephesus. In Christian tradition, Mary is believed to have spent her final years here; that tradition should not be presented as archaeological proof that she lived at this site. The small chapel and its surroundings are a place of significance for Christian and Muslim visitors today. This is not the same place as the Church of Mary inside the ancient city. It is one of the components of the UNESCO Ephesus World Heritage property. The map pin approximately represents the building, not the visitor entrance. Check transport, admission and current visiting conditions before you go.

=== agora-tapinagi ===
A temple once stood in the centre of the State Agora, adding a religious dimension to this civic space. Some sources call it the Temple of Isis, while others regard its dedication as uncertain. We therefore do not treat that identification as established fact. The remaining foundations help us imagine the ancient square, but the map pin represents the approximate site rather than an entrance or a walking route.

=== yamac-ev-1 ===
Terrace House 1 is one of the two residential blocks on the slope south of Curetes Street. Its terraced rooms and housing units reveal the variety of domestic architecture in Ephesus. It is distinct from Terrace House 2, although both belong to the wider Terrace Houses area. The map pin approximately represents the building area, not a visitor entrance. Please check current access before visiting.

=== hadrian-kapisi ===
Hadrian’s Gate is a monumental gateway near the Library of Celsus, at the western end of Curetes Street. Also called the New Triodos Gate, it marked a meeting point of streets in the ancient city. Its surviving remains belonged to an elaborate structure that once rose through several storeys. Do not confuse it with the Temple of Hadrian elsewhere on Curetes Street. The map pin shows an approximate location, not an entrance or a safe walking route.

=== alytarkhos-stoasi ===
The so-called Alytarchs’ Stoa was a colonnaded hall on the south side of Curetes Street, in front of the Terrace Houses. Because of the slope it had two levels, with shops and workshops behind it. Surviving mosaics with geometric and plant designs recall the richness of this ancient street. Its name comes from an inscription referring to an official responsible for rules at athletic contests. The map pin represents the approximate centre of the long structure, not an entrance or safe walking route.

=== ask-evi ===
Near the Library of Celsus is a group of remains long called the House of Pleasure or brothel. The archaeological site sign explains that this was a mistaken interpretation, and describes private houses with courtyards and shops opening onto the street. The remains of the building complex help us understand domestic life in Roman Ephesus. This is not the same residential block as Terrace House Two. The map pin represents the wider area approximately, not an entrance or walking route.

=== tiyatro-cesmesi ===
The Hellenistic fountain house near the stage building of the Great Theatre is an example of the water architecture of ancient Ephesus. Ionic columns at its front and carefully worked marble are among its distinctive features. The building was also altered in Roman times. It should not be confused with another Hellenistic fountain near the Terrace Houses. The map pin shows an approximate location. Sources differ on its position, and it is not a field survey.

=== pollio-domitian-cesmesi ===
At the western edge of Domitian Square rises a memorial tomb built of ashlar blocks. It was erected in memory of Gaius Sextilius Pollio, a wealthy citizen of Ephesus, by his stepson. In this way the city honoured the man who had donated the Basilica on the State Agora and the aqueducts that brought water to the city. All that survives of the structure is its socle, eight by six and a half metres and some six and a half metres high. Together with the fountain later added beside it, it forms a single architectural screen facing the square.

=== laecanius-cesmesi ===
At the southwest corner of the State Agora stood a monumental water building known as the Fountain of Laecanius Bassus, or Hydrekdocheion. It was donated by the proconsul Gaius Laecanius Bassus around 80 CE. Ornate façades framed its rectangular pool on three sides, and water flowing from sculptures added movement to the architecture. It is distinct from another fountain along the southern side of the agora. The map pin approximately represents the structure, not an entrance or a route.

=== hydreion-kalkidikum ===
At the western end of Curetes Street, right beside the Monument of Memmius, stands a fountain house with a semicircular plan. Known in excavation reports as the Hydreion, this nymphaeum was a visible node in the city's water network, with its niche and two basins facing the street. Four pedestals in front of the niche carried statues of the Tetrarchs, which shows the building was still in use in the late third century CE. The date of its first phase is disputed: some sources point to the Augustan period, others to the late second century CE. The remains now lie below street level, so they are hard to make out without looking closely.

=== triodos-meydani ===
This small square, where Curetes Street, Marble Street and the Ortygia road meet, is the ancient city's "three roads". The façade of the Library of Celsus rises to the west, and Curetes Street passes to the north. Its grandest monument is the three-storey gate now called Hadrian's Gate; it was originally known as the Triodos Gate, and its central arch was wider than the side ones. Repaired after the earthquake of 262, the gate framed ceremonial entries into the city. The identity of the round monument on the square is debated: one view calls it a sundial, another a tomb. The pin sits at the centre of the square, very close to the gate and library pins.

=== heksagon ===
East of the Octagon and west of the Stoa of Alytarches, beneath the steps of Curetes Street, lies a small monument with a hexagonal plan. Uncovered in the 1994 excavation, it was almost completely buried when the street level was raised, so it is hard to pick out as a visitor. Its function is not certain; it is read together with the neighbouring upper-city monuments, the Octagon and the Heroon. Its name describes only its plan, not its identity. The position is approximated from the west-to-east sequence Heroon–Octagon–Hexagon; it is not a point measured on site. The photograph shows the street, not the monument.

=== verulanus-meydani-salonu ===
The largest athletic area of the harbour district was a colonnaded court about 200 by 240 metres. It takes its name from Verulanus, high priest of Asia under Hadrian. Its floor was paved in thirteen different shades of marble, and the surrounding colonnaded walk (xystos) was where athletes trained. The Harbour Baths lie to its east, the Church of Mary — also called the harbour church — to its west. The court's edges are now under soil, so the pin is representative: it marks the eastern corner beside the Church of Mary. The point given in the sources overlaps the Harbour Baths, so it was not used as it stands.

=== antik-liman-kanali ===
Ephesus was a harbour city; today the ancient harbour lies about five kilometres inland, on flat ground filled with river silt. As the mud carried by the Kaystros cut the harbour off from the city, the basin was moved several times and finally abandoned. Harbour Street (the Arcadiane) ran straight to the harbour, and a channel connected the basin to the sea. Because of marsh and drainage problems the area is not a safe walking route today; the pin marks the centre of the basin, not an entrance. The photograph shows the plain seen from Bülbül Mountain rather than the basin itself.

=== meryem-kilisesi-vaftizhanesi ===
In the courtyard north of the Church of Mary stands an octagonal baptistery. The building has four entrances, and at its centre is a multi-sided font reached by steps on two sides; it counts as one of the best-preserved fonts of this size in Turkey. Floor panels inscribed in Greek and Latin record phases of repair. The baptistery belongs to the church that hosted the Council of Ephesus in 431, though its exact date ranges between the fourth and sixth centuries in the sources. The position comes from the photograph showing the northern part of the courtyard; it is not a separately published coordinate.

=== bizans-hamamlari ===
North of the lower entrance, west of the street joining the stadium and the theatre, stands a large late-antique complex about 75 by 50 metres. Excavations in the 1950s debated a bath, a governor's palace and a church on the same spot; the current reading points to a multi-phase complex rather than a single function. An octagonal hall, a broad courtyard and mosaic corridors are among its parts. The name "Baths of Constantine" belongs not to this building but to the Harbour Baths; the confusion is common in older literature. The pin is Vici's bath point, about 25 metres from the palace point.

=== vedius-gymnasionu ===
North of the stadium, just inside the city wall, stands the best-studied bath-gymnasium complex of Ephesus. Publius Vedius Antoninus and his wife Flavia Papiane had it built in 147–149, and its first phase covered an area of 135 by 85 metres. The colossal statue of Kaystros found in the frigidarium is now in the Izmir Museum; the photograph here shows that statue, not the building. The dedication is inconsistent in the sources: one record names Aphrodite, another Artemis together with Antoninus Pius. The site is closed to visitors, so the pin is representative, and the sources differ by about 90 metres.

=== kent-surlari ===
When Lysimachos refounded Ephesus in the early third century BCE he enclosed the city with a wall more than nine kilometres long; over three kilometres of that line still stand. The wall followed the slopes of Panayır and Bülbül mountains, protecting the harbour and the upper city. In the sixth century the Byzantine emperor Justinian left traces of repair and rebuilding. The point marked here is not a gate or an entrance but a preserved stretch of wall on the hillside; the whole circuit cannot be walked as a single stop. The coordinate comes from the location recorded for the Commons photograph.

=== chalcidicum ===
At the western edge of Domitian Square stands a massive substructure that carried the western end of the Basilica Stoa. Its name comes from the Latin chalcidicum, a porticoed hall added to a basilica. Built of large ashlar blocks, it was added in the reign of Nero to support the western end of the stoa. Its interior was divided into three chambers, entered through doorways opening directly onto the square. The structure projects fifteen point nine metres into the square, and its outer walls were built of stone brought from Mount Panayır. In late antiquity a small church was installed inside it. The mass itself survives; nothing of its upper decoration remains.

=== domitian-cesmesi ===
Just south of the Pollio Monument rises a fountain built in 92/93 CE, its great apse opening directly onto Domitian Square. Water carried by the arches on tendril-carved piers was distributed through a branching network of fired clay pipes. In the niche stood a sculptural group showing Odysseus blinding the one-eyed Polyphemus; the group was not made for this fountain but brought here from another building. The structure absorbed the older Pollio Monument, creating a single architectural screen. After the emperor's death his name was chiselled out of the inscription here as well.
